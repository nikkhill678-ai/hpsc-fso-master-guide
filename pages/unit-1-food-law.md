## MODULE 1: FOOD HAZARDS & FOODBORNE ILLNESSES

### 1.1 First-Principles: Why does this module exist?
Before memorising definitions, build the mental model[cite: 7]. Every food safety question ultimately reduces to one chain[cite: 7]:

> **SOURCE** (raw material / environment / handler)  
> ➔ **HAZARD** enters food (biological / chemical / physical)  
> ➔ **HAZARD** survives processing / storage  
> ➔ **HAZARD** reaches consumer in a dose sufficient to harm  
> ➔ **ADVERSE HEALTH EFFECT** (illness, injury, chronic disease)[cite: 7]

Food safety science is simply the discipline of breaking this chain at the earliest possible point[cite: 7]. Everything in this module — definitions, hazard classes, infection vs intoxication, the WHO Five Keys — is organised around where in this chain you intervene[cite: 7].

### 1.2 Codified Definitions (verbatim-grade, exam-ready)

| Term | Definition (exam-ready) | Conceptual Anchor |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Food Safety** | Assurance that food will not cause harm to the consumer when prepared and/or eaten according to its intended use[cite: 7]. | Outcome-based guarantee[cite: 7] |
| **Toxicity** | The capacity of a substance to produce harm or injury of any kind, under any condition of exposure[cite: 7]. | Intrinsic property of the substance[cite: 7] |
| **Hazard (Codex)** | "A biological, chemical or physical agent in, or condition of, food with the potential to cause an adverse health effect."[cite: 7] | Potential, not certainty[cite: 7] |
| **Contamination** | Presence of harmful or objectionable foreign substances (chemicals, micro-organisms, dilutants) in food, before/during/after processing or storage[cite: 7]. | Unintended presence[cite: 7] |
| **Adulteration** | Intentional or accidental addition of impure, cheap, or unnecessary ingredients to cheat, cheapen, or falsify a preparation, altering composition/quality[cite: 7]. | Deliberate or negligent degradation[cite: 7] |
| **Food Quality** | Attributes that influence a product's value to the consumer — negative (spoilage, contamination, adulteration, safety hazards) AND positive (colour, flavour, texture)[cite: 7]. | Quality includes Safety (quality is the wider umbrella)[cite: 7] |

**Toxicity vs Hazard** — the single most tested distinction[cite: 7]. Toxicity is a fixed intrinsic property (e.g., cyanide is always toxic)[cite: 7]. Hazard is a contextual probability — it depends on dose, exposure route, and whether the substance is "not used in the prescribed manner and quantity."[cite: 7] A substance can be toxic but pose negligible hazard if controlled (e.g., nitrite at legal ppm levels)[cite: 7].

> **🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP (Absolute Qualifier Alert):**  
> A question may state *"Toxicity and Hazard are synonymous terms under Codex."* This is **FALSE**[cite: 7]. Toxicity = capacity to cause harm (static)[cite: 7]. Hazard = probability of harm occurring given actual use conditions (dynamic/contextual)[cite: 7]. Never conflate the two on MCQ elimination logic[cite: 7].

### 1.3 Tripartite Classification of Hazards

**FOOD SAFETY HAZARDS**[cite: 7]
* **BIOLOGICAL**[cite: 7]
  * Macro-biological (visible)[cite: 7]
  * Micro-biological (invisible: bacteria, yeast, mold, protozoa, viruses)[cite: 7]
* **CHEMICAL**[cite: 7]
  * Agri-chemicals (pesticides, hormones, antibiotics)[cite: 7]
  * Pollution (heavy metals: Pb, Hg, As, Cd)[cite: 7]
  * Food chemicals / naturally occurring toxicants[cite: 7]
* **PHYSICAL**[cite: 7]
  * Extraneous matter / foreign bodies[cite: 7]

#### (A) Biological Hazards
**Intuition:** Micro-organisms are everywhere; they only become a "hazard" the moment they are (i) pathogenic to humans AND (ii) present in food in numbers/toxin-load sufficient to cause illness[cite: 7]. Spoilage organisms do not equal pathogenic organisms — a food can look/smell fine and still carry a biological hazard (classic trap)[cite: 7].
* **Macro-biological hazards:** visible pests and insects — larvae, adult insects, rodent droppings/hair — detectable by the naked eye[cite: 7].
* **Micro-biological hazards** (invisible without magnification)[cite: 7]:
  * Bacteria (e.g., *Salmonella*, *Clostridium botulinum*, *Staphylococcus aureus*)[cite: 7]
  * Yeasts and moulds (spoilage + some mycotoxin producers)[cite: 7]
  * Protozoa (parasitic)[cite: 7]
  * Viruses (do not multiply in food, but survive and transmit via faecal-oral route)[cite: 7]

#### (B) Chemical Hazards
| Sub-category | Examples | Entry Point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Agricultural chemicals** | Pesticides, fungicides, fertilizers, growth hormones, antibiotics[cite: 7] | Primary production (crop/livestock)[cite: 7] |
| **Environmental pollutants** | Heavy metals — Lead (Pb), Mercury (Hg), Arsenic (As), Cadmium (Cd)[cite: 7] | Industrial discharge ➔ bioaccumulation along food chain, especially marine life[cite: 7] |
| **Plant/equipment chemicals** | Cleaning agents, lubricants, sanitizer residues[cite: 7] | Improper cleaning of food-contact surfaces[cite: 7] |
| **Naturally occurring toxicants**| Mycotoxins (aflatoxins), glycoalkaloids, cyanogenic glycosides[cite: 7] | Intrinsic to the food/crop (covered fully in Module 2)[cite: 7] |
| **Food chemicals (additives)** | ~2,500+ chemicals used for preservation/flavour/appearance[cite: 7] | Deliberately added, but hazard arises if controls fail[cite: 7] |
| **Food allergens** | Protein-based — see Big 8 below[cite: 7] | Natural constituents mislabelled/cross-contaminated[cite: 7] |

> **Bioaccumulation mnemonic for heavy metals:** **"PHAC"** ➔ **P**b (Lead), **H**g (Mercury), **A**s (Arsenic), **C**d (Cadmium)[cite: 7]. These concentrate progressively up the food chain (biomagnification) — mercury in large predatory fish is the textbook case[cite: 7].

#### (C) Physical Hazards
Defined as any physical material not normally found in food, with potential to cause illness or injury[cite: 7].

| Material | Typical Source | Injury Potential |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Glass** | Bottles, jars, light fixtures, gauge covers[cite: 7] | Cuts, bleeding, may need surgical removal[cite: 7] |
| **Wood** | Fields, pallets, boxes, buildings[cite: 7] | Cuts, infection, choking, may need surgery[cite: 7] |
| **Stones** | Fields, buildings[cite: 7] | Choking, broken teeth[cite: 7] |
| **Metal** | Machinery, fields, wire, employees[cite: 7] | Cuts, infection, may need surgery[cite: 7] |
| **Insects** | Fields, plant, post-process entry[cite: 7] | Illness, trauma, choking[cite: 7] |

> **🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP (Physical Hazards vs Defects):**  
> A question may ask: *"Which of the following is NEVER a HACCP-relevant physical hazard: insects / glass / hair / metal fragments?"* Remember from Module 6: hair, filth, and simple insect presence without injury potential are classified as **"undesirable defects," NOT HACCP hazards**, unless they carry a direct injury/illness mechanism[cite: 7].

#### (D) Food Allergens — The "Big 8"
Statutory allergen list (protein-derived)[cite: 7]:
1. Milk[cite: 7]
2. Egg[cite: 7]
3. Fish[cite: 7]
4. Crustacean shellfish[cite: 7]
5. Tree nuts[cite: 7]
6. Wheat[cite: 7]
7. Peanuts[cite: 7]
8. Soybeans[cite: 7]

These are protein-derived hazards — the allergenic potential resides in specific proteins, not the whole food matrix[cite: 7]. Highly refined oils carry lower allergenic risk than the whole legume, though labelling precautions still apply[cite: 7].

### 1.4 Food Infection vs Food Intoxication — THE Definitive Distinction
**First-Principles Intuition:** Ask: *"Is the live organism itself the problem, or is a pre-formed poison the problem?"*[cite: 7]
* **Infection** = you swallow a living pathogen population that invades and multiplies inside you[cite: 7]. Damage accrues over hours to days[cite: 7].
* **Intoxication** = you swallow a poison that is already made (toxin), whether or not the organism that made it is still alive[cite: 7]. Damage begins almost immediately[cite: 7].

| Parameter | Food Infection | Food Intoxication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cause** | Live pathogenic bacteria / viruses / parasites[cite: 7] | Pre-formed toxin (bacterial, chemical, or natural)[cite: 7] |
| **Mechanism** | Invasion and/or multiplication within intestinal lining or tissues[cite: 7] | NO invasion or multiplication required; toxin acts directly[cite: 7] |
| **Incubation period**| **Hours to days** (time needed to multiply)[cite: 7] | **Minutes to hours** (toxin acts fast)[cite: 7] |
| **Classic symptoms** | Diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, **fever** (sign of immune response)[cite: 7] | Diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, double vision, respiratory failure, **fever typically absent**[cite: 7] |
| **Transmissibility** | **CAN spread** person-to-person via faecal-oral route[cite: 7] | **NOT communicable**[cite: 7] |
| **Key factors** | Inadequate cooking, cross-contamination, poor personal hygiene[cite: 7] | Time-temperature abuse allowing toxin formation prior to eating[cite: 7] |
| **Classic examples** | *Salmonella* spp.[cite: 7] | *Staphylococcus aureus* enterotoxin, *Clostridium botulinum* toxin[cite: 7] |

> **🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP (Fever & Transmissibility):**  
> **"Fever" is a high-yield discriminator[cite: 7].** Infections typically present WITH fever; intoxications typically present WITHOUT fever but WITH neurological signs[cite: 7]. If an MCQ gives a symptom set with double vision + respiratory failure + NO fever ➔ answer is intoxication (botulism)[cite: 7]. If it gives fever + cramps + multi-day onset ➔ answer is infection (*Salmonella*)[cite: 7].  
> **Absolute qualifier:** *"Food intoxication is ALWAYS communicable."* — **FALSE**[cite: 7].

### 1.5 Food-Borne Disease: Classification & Transmission Routes
**Food-borne disease outbreak:** occurrence of 2 or more cases of similar illness from a common food source, OR observed cases exceeding the expected baseline number[cite: 7].

**Transmission Pathways (chain-of-custody view):**[cite: 7]
* Infected food handler ➔ *faecal-oral / nasal flora* ➔ ready-to-eat food[cite: 7]
* Cross-contamination ➔ *shared utensils/surfaces* ➔ uninfected food[cite: 7]
* Inadequate cooking ➔ *survives heat-stable toxins* ➔ consumer[cite: 7]
* Improper storage (10–50°C "danger zone") ➔ *pathogen multiplication* ➔ consumer[cite: 7]

### 1.6 WHO Five Keys to Safer Food (Operational Framework)

| Key | Operational Guidance |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1. Keep clean** | Wash hands before handling food and often during preparation; sanitize all surfaces; protect kitchen areas from insects/pests[cite: 7]. |
| **2. Separate raw & cooked** | Separate raw meat, poultry, and seafood from other foods; use separate equipment/utensils (cutting boards)[cite: 7]. |
| **3. Cook thoroughly** | Cook food thoroughly (meat, eggs, seafood); bring soups/stews to boiling to ensure pathogen destruction; reheat thoroughly[cite: 7]. |
| **4. Keep food at safe temperatures** | Do not leave cooked food at room temp for more than 2 hours; refrigerate promptly (preferably below 5°C); keep cooked food hot (above 60°C); do not thaw at room temp[cite: 7]. |
| **5. Use safe water & raw materials** | Use safe water; select fresh/wholesome foods; choose processed foods (e.g., pasteurized milk); wash raw fruits/vegetables; respect expiry dates[cite: 7]. |

*(Mnemonic: C-S-C-T-W ➔ Clean, Separate, Cook, Temperature, Water)*[cite: 7]

## MODULE 2: FOOD ADDITIVES & PLANT-ORIGIN TOXICANTS

### 2.1 First-Principles: Why classify additives?
Regulators distinguish between substances whose safety is self-evident from traditional use (Class I) versus substances that carry a dose-dependent risk profile requiring a legal ceiling (Class II)[cite: 7].

| Class I (Natural, unrestricted\*) | Class II (Chemical, legally capped) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Common salt[cite: 7] | Benzoic acid & its salts[cite: 7] |
| Sugar, dextrose/glucose syrup[cite: 7] | Sulphurous acid & its sulphites[cite: 7] |
| Wood smoke, Spices[cite: 7] | Nitrites & nitrates[cite: 7] |
| Vinegar / acetic acid, Honey[cite: 7] | Sorbic acid & its salts[cite: 7] |

*\*unless otherwise provided in the Rules*[cite: 7]

> **🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP (Unrestricted Class I):**  
> **Absolute qualifier:** *"Class I preservatives are permitted in UNLIMITED quantity in ALL foods."* — **FALSE**[cite: 7]. Class I is "not restricted *unless otherwise provided in the Rules*" — a conditional exemption[cite: 7].

### 2.2 Additive Categories — Mechanism Walkthrough

#### (A) Antioxidants
**Mechanism:** Fats/oils undergo autoxidation (free-radical chain reaction) converting unsaturated fatty acids into aldehydes/ketones (rancidity)[cite: 7]. Antioxidants donate a hydrogen atom to the lipid free radical, terminating the chain reaction[cite: 7].
* **Natural:** Tocopherols (Vitamin E)[cite: 7]. Heat-labile[cite: 7].
* **Synthetic:** BHA, BHT[cite: 7]. BHA is highly effective for short-chain fatty acids[cite: 7].

#### (B) Emulsifiers & Stabilizers
**Mechanism:** Amphiphilic molecules (hydrophilic head + lipophilic tail) sit at the oil-water interface to stabilize two-phase systems[cite: 7].
* **HLB (Hydrophilic-Lipophilic Balance):** Determines whether it favors oil-in-water or water-in-oil emulsions[cite: 7].
* **Examples:** Monoglycerides (most widely used), Lecithin[cite: 7].

#### (C) Clarifying Agents
Used to remove chill haze (tannins/polyphenols) in beverages[cite: 7]. Examples: Gelatin, PVP (polyvinylpyrrolidone)[cite: 7].

#### (D) Colorants
* **Natural:** Carotenoid, annatto, anthocyanin, caramel, chlorophyll, turmeric[cite: 7].
* **Nature-Identical Synthetic:** Lab-synthesized replicas[cite: 7].
* **Artificial:** Coal-tar dyes (e.g., Indigocarmine)[cite: 7].
* **Inorganic:** **Titanium Dioxide** (capped at 1% in CHEWING GUM only)[cite: 7].

> **🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP (Titanium Dioxide):**  
> Titanium dioxide is permitted at **1% in chewing gum ONLY**[cite: 7]. Any MCQ substituting a different percentage (e.g., 2%) or a different food (e.g., "all confectionery") is a trap[cite: 7].

#### (E) Flavour Enhancers & Sweeteners
* **MSG:** Enhances/synergizes flavours (umami)[cite: 7].
* **5′-ribonucleotides:** ~10× the flavour-enhancing property of MSG, acts synergistically[cite: 7].
* **Non-nutritive sweeteners:** Saccharin (~300× sweeter than sucrose), aspartame, acesulfame-K[cite: 7].

#### (F) Anticaking Agents & Antibiotics
* **Anticaking:** Calcium silicate (table salt, up to 2%), Microcrystalline cellulose[cite: 7].
* **Antibiotics (Nisin):** Polypeptide antibiotic produced by streptococci[cite: 7]. Prevents outgrowth of *Clostridium botulinum* in processed cheese[cite: 7]. **Active against Gram-positive organisms only[cite: 7].**

### 2.4 Endogenous Plant Toxicants & Anti-Nutrients

#### (A) Glycoalkaloids — α-Chaconine & α-Solanine
* **Source:** Solanaceae plants (potatoes, tomatoes)[cite: 7].
* **Limit:** 200 mg/kg (potatoes)[cite: 7].
* **Detoxification:** **Thermally stable[cite: 7].** Baking/frying do NOT reduce content significantly[cite: 7]. **Peeling** is the primary method (removes up to 60%)[cite: 7]. Tomatine in tomatoes degrades as the fruit ripens[cite: 7].

#### (B) Cyanogenic Glycosides
* **Mechanism:** Tissue damage brings the glycoside and endogenous plant enzyme (β-glycosidase) into contact ➔ Releases cyanohydrin ➔ Decomposes to **Hydrogen Cyanide (HCN)**[cite: 7].
* **Sources:** Linamarin (Cassava), Amygdalin (Stone fruit pits/almonds)[cite: 7].
* **Detoxification:** Chopping, soaking in running water, fermentation, cooking (allows HCN to volatilize/leach out)[cite: 7].

#### (C) Lectins (Haemagglutinins)
* **Mechanism:** Carbohydrate-binding glycoproteins[cite: 7]. Survive GI digestion, bind to gut mucosal receptors causing damage[cite: 7].
* **Sources:** Legumes (Red kidney beans)[cite: 7].
* **Detoxification:** Highly stable, requires vigorous boiling[cite: 7].

#### (D) Anti-Nutrient Mnemonic Table
| Toxicant | Plant Family |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Glycoalkaloids** | Solanaceae (Potato/Tomato)[cite: 7] |
| **Glucosinolates** | Cruciferae (Cabbage/Mustard)[cite: 7] |
| **Cucurbitacins** | Cucurbitaceae (Gourds)[cite: 7] |
| **Lectins / Proteinase Inhibitors** | Leguminosae (Beans/Legumes)[cite: 7] |

## MODULE 3: MICROBIOLOGICAL PRINCIPLES & SPOILAGE CRITERIA

### 3.1 The Three-Lever Control Model
Every microorganism needs three things to grow: **Temperature**, **pH**, and **Water Activity (aw)**[cite: 7].

#### 1. Cardinal Parameter: Temperature Domains
* **Psychrotrophs:** Grow well at 7°C or below (Optimum: 20–30°C)[cite: 7]
* **Mesophiles:** Grow well at 20–45°C (Optimum: 30–40°C)[cite: 7]
* **Thermophiles:** Grow well at 45°C or above (Optimum: 55–65°C)[cite: 7]

#### 2. Cardinal Parameter: pH Thresholds
* **Most organisms:** near pH 7.0[cite: 7]
* **Lactic acid bacteria:** can grow as low as pH 2.0[cite: 7]
* **Clostridium botulinum:** **Cannot grow below pH 4.6**[cite: 7]

> **THE Critical Public Health Threshold:**  
> pH 4.6 is the universal cutoff differentiating Low-acid foods (pH above 4.6, require full retort sterilization) from Acid foods (pH 4.6 or below, safer from *C. botulinum*)[cite: 7].

#### 3. Cardinal Parameter: Water Activity (aw)
* Fresh produce: aw above 0.99[cite: 7]
* Spoilage bacteria: aw above 0.91[cite: 7]
* Filamentous fungi / moulds: can grow at aw as low as **0.80**[cite: 7]

### 3.5 Spoilage Comparison Summary
| Organism Group | Competitive Niche | Key Genera |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bacteria** | Neutral pH, moderate-high aw[cite: 7] | Soft rot bacteria[cite: 7] |
| **Yeasts** | High sugar/salt, low pH[cite: 7] | *Zygosaccharomyces*, *Candida*[cite: 7] |
| **Filamentous fungi** | Low aw (down to 0.80), wide pH tolerance[cite: 7] | Various moulds (Obligate aerobes)[cite: 7] |

## MODULE 4: POST-HARVEST ADULTERATION

### 4.2 Artificial Ripening Agents
**Calcium Carbide ("Masala") — STRICTLY BANNED**[cite: 7]
* **Mechanism:** CaC2 + Moisture ➔ Acetylene gas (C2H2)[cite: 7]. Acetylene mimics ethylene superficially[cite: 7].
* **Why Banned:** Trace contamination with toxic **arsenic and phosphorus hydride**[cite: 7]. Produces superficial colouration without internal ripening[cite: 7].
* **Safe Alternative:** Ethephon / Ethylene Gas[cite: 7].

### 4.3 Hazardous Dyes & Chemical Malpractices
| Chemical | Commodity Target | Toxic Effect / Detection |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Malachite Green** | Bitter gourd, green chili[cite: 7] | Carcinogenic; **Blotting Paper Test**[cite: 7] |
| **Copper sulfate** | Lady fingers[cite: 7] | Anaemia, abdominal pain[cite: 7] |
| **Methanil yellow / Lead chromate** | Watermelons[cite: 7] | Neurotoxic, reproductive degeneration[cite: 7] |
| **Oxytocin** | Cucurbits/gourds[cite: 7] | Mammalian hormone misused for size enlargement[cite: 7] |

### 4.4 Food-Grade Waxing Regulations
* **Approved Waxes:** Carnauba wax, Beeswax, Shellac wax[cite: 7].
* **NOT Allowed:** Mineral oils or unapproved petroleum waxes[cite: 7].
* **Labelling:** Waxed produce MUST have a clear label declaration stating the origin of the wax[cite: 7].

## MODULE 5: PACKAGING & LABELLING STATUTORY STANDARDS

### 5.2 Statutory Date Markings
* **"Best before":** Signifies the end of the period for peak quality[cite: 7]. Food MAY still be perfectly safe to consume post-date unless independently unsafe[cite: 7].
* **"Use by / Expiry date":** Absolute safety cutoff[cite: 7]. Food SHALL NOT be sold after this date[cite: 7].

### 5.3 Vegetarian vs Non-Vegetarian Food
> **🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP (The Milk Exclusion):**  
> **"Non-Vegetarian Food"** legally means food containing whole/part of any animal/bird/egg, **BUT EXCLUDING MILK OR MILK PRODUCTS**[cite: 7]. Milk is statutorily classified as VEGETARIAN under this legal definition[cite: 7].

### 5.4 Packaging Material Safety
* **Prohibited Containers:** Rusty containers, enameled chipped containers, untinned copper/brass (copper toxicity)[cite: 7].
* **Tomato Ketchup/Sauces:** Clean bottles[cite: 7]. Open-top sanitary cans may be used **ONLY IF acidity does not exceed 0.5% as acetic acid**[cite: 7].

## MODULE 6: THE HACCP SYSTEM & QUALITY ASSURANCE

### 6.1 First-Principles
HACCP shifts control upstream from end-product testing to real-time process monitoring[cite: 7].
* **Origin:** Pillsbury Company (1960s) for NASA[cite: 7].
* **Formalization:** NACMCF (1985)[cite: 7].

### 6.3 Hazard vs Undesirable Defect
In HACCP, "hazard" means potential to cause illness/injury[cite: 7].
* **Undesirable Defects:** Hair, filth, spoilage, economic fraud, insect presence (without direct injury potential) are NOT HACCP hazards[cite: 7].

### 6.4 The 7 Core HACCP Principles
1. **Conduct Hazard Analysis** (differentiate safety vs quality)[cite: 7].
2. **Determine CCPs** (Critical Control Points using decision tree logic)[cite: 7].
3. **Establish Critical Limits** (boundaries for safety)[cite: 7].
4. **Establish CCP Monitoring Procedures** (What, How, Frequency, Who)[cite: 7].
5. **Establish Corrective Actions** (when a critical limit is violated)[cite: 7].
6. **Establish Verification Procedures** (Validation happens *before*, Verification is *ongoing*)[cite: 7].
7. **Establish Documentation and Record-Keeping**[cite: 7].

### 6.5 CCP vs Prerequisite Programme (PRP)
| Feature | Critical Control Point (CCP) | Prerequisite Programme (PRP) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scope** | Specific point directly controlling a significant hazard[cite: 7] | General, plant-wide foundational practices (GMP, GHP, sanitation)[cite: 7] |
| **Monitoring** | Continuous/frequent, tied to a measurable limit[cite: 7] | Periodic inspection/audit[cite: 7] |
| **Examples** | Pasteurization temp/time; Metal detector[cite: 7] | Water potability; pest control[cite: 7] |

> **🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP (CCP Decision Logic):**  
> **Absolute qualifier:** *"Every step that controls a hazard is automatically a CCP."* — **FALSE**[cite: 7]. If a LATER step in the process will adequately eliminate/reduce the SAME hazard to an acceptable level, the EARLIER step is not designated a CCP[cite: 7].

### STAGE 2: DESCRIPTIVE MODEL ANSWER (15 MARKS)
**Question: Elaborate the 7 Principles of Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point (HACCP). Explain how a Critical Control Point (CCP) is determined and distinguished from a Prerequisite Programme (PRP).**[cite: 7]

#### 1. Introduction & Historical Foundations (1 mark)
HACCP is a proactive, preventative food safety management system designed to identify and control hazards rather than relying on retrospective finished product testing[cite: 7]. Pioneered in the early 1960s by the Pillsbury Company alongside NASA to ensure absolute food safety for manned space flights, the system was formally codified into 7 core principles by the National Advisory Committee on Microbiological Criteria for Foods (NACMCF) in 1985[cite: 7].

#### 2. The 7 Core HACCP Principles (9 marks)
* **Principle 1: Conduct Hazard Analysis:** The HACCP team identifies and assesses all potential biological, chemical, and physical hazards capable of causing consumer illness or injury[cite: 7]. Crucially, safety concerns are separated from quality defects (such as hair or filth, which do not pose direct health hazards)[cite: 7].
* **Principle 2: Determine Critical Control Points (CCPs):** A CCP is a specific point or step in a food process where control can be applied to prevent, eliminate, or reduce a food safety hazard to an acceptable level[cite: 7]. CCPs are systematically determined using structured **Decision Tree logic**[cite: 7].
* **Principle 3: Establish Critical Limits:** For each CCP, measurable boundaries (criteria that must be met) are defined to differentiate between safe and unsafe operating conditions (e.g., thermal boundaries, time, pH, water activity)[cite: 7].
* **Principle 4: Establish Monitoring Procedures:** A scheduled sequence of observations or measurements is established to ensure the CCP remains within critical limits[cite: 7]. Effective monitoring protocols address the four fundamentals: **What** is monitored, **How** it is monitored, **Frequency**, and **Who** carries out the monitoring[cite: 7].
* **Principle 5: Establish Corrective Actions:** Predetermined, documented protocols executed immediately when monitoring indicates a deviation from an established critical limit[cite: 7]. They must: (1) restore the process to control, and (2) segregate, evaluate, and determine the safe disposition of affected product[cite: 7].
* **Principle 6: Establish Verification Procedures:** The application of methods, procedures, tests, and other evaluations to confirm compliance with the HACCP plan[cite: 7]. This incorporates **Validation** (scientific substantiation conducted upfront prior to implementation) and ongoing **Verification** (audits, calibrations, review of monitoring logs)[cite: 7].
* **Principle 7: Establish Documentation and Record-Keeping:** Maintenance of complete records demonstrating safe manufacture[cite: 7]. This includes support documentation for critical limits, CCP monitoring logs, corrective action reports, and verification/audit results[cite: 7].

#### 3. Determination of a CCP via Decision Tree (3 marks)
A process step is designated as a CCP by asking a sequence of logical questions[cite: 7]:
1. Does a control measure exist at this step for the identified hazard[cite: 7]?
2. Is control at this step necessary for safety[cite: 7]?
3. Is the hazard likely to occur above acceptable levels or increase to an unacceptable level at this step[cite: 7]?
4. **Will a subsequent step eliminate or reduce the hazard to an acceptable level[cite: 7]?**  
   * *If YES* ➔ The step is **NOT a CCP** (the hazard will be eliminated downstream)[cite: 7].  
   * *If NO* ➔ **THIS STEP IS A CCP**[cite: 7].

#### 4. CCP vs Prerequisite Programme (PRP) Distinction (2 marks)

| Parameter | Critical Control Point (CCP) | Prerequisite Programme (PRP) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scope** | Point-specific and hazard-specific (e.g., pasteurization holding tube)[cite: 7]. | Broad, plant-wide foundational hygiene (e.g., GMP, GHP, pest control, water potability)[cite: 7]. |
| **Derivation** | Derived through hazard analysis and Decision Tree logic[cite: 7]. | Universal baseline requirements independent of specific product flows[cite: 7]. |
| **Criteria** | Controlled by strict, measurable numerical Critical Limits[cite: 7]. | Monitored through general audits, inspection checklists, and SOP compliance[cite: 7]. |
| **Deviation Consequence** | Poses immediate direct food safety hazard; mandates immediate product isolation and corrective disposition[cite: 7]. | Addressed via routine facility maintenance and corrective sanitation without necessarily halting batches[cite: 7]. |
