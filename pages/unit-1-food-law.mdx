## MODULE 1: FOOD HAZARDS & FOODBORNE ILLNESSES

### 1.1 First-Principles: Why does this module exist?
Before memorising definitions, build the mental model. Every food safety question ultimately reduces to one chain:

> **SOURCE** (raw material / environment / handler) ➔ 
> **HAZARD** enters food (biological / chemical / physical) ➔ 
> **HAZARD** survives processing / storage ➔ 
> **HAZARD** reaches consumer in a dose sufficient to harm ➔ 
> **ADVERSE HEALTH EFFECT** (illness, injury, chronic disease)

Food safety science is simply the discipline of breaking this chain at the earliest possible point. Everything in this module — definitions, hazard classes, infection vs intoxication, the WHO Five Keys — is organised around where in this chain you intervene.

### 1.2 Codified Definitions (verbatim-grade, exam-ready)

| Term | Definition (exam-ready) | Conceptual Anchor |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Food Safety** | Assurance that food will not cause harm to the consumer when prepared and/or eaten according to its intended use. | Outcome-based guarantee |
| **Toxicity** | The capacity of a substance to produce harm or injury of any kind, under any condition of exposure. | Intrinsic property of the substance |
| **Hazard** *(Codex)* | "A biological, chemical or physical agent in, or condition of, food with the potential to cause an adverse health effect." | Potential, not certainty |
| **Contamination** | Presence of harmful or objectionable foreign substances (chemicals, micro-organisms, dilutants) in food, before/during/after processing or storage. | Unintended presence |
| **Adulteration** | Intentional or accidental addition of impure, cheap, or unnecessary ingredients to cheat, cheapen, or falsify a preparation, altering composition/quality. | Deliberate or negligent degradation |
| **Food Quality** | Attributes that influence a product's value to the consumer — negative (spoilage, contamination, adulteration, safety hazards) AND positive (colour, flavour, texture). | Quality ⊃ Safety (quality is the wider umbrella) |

**Toxicity vs Hazard** — the single most tested distinction. Toxicity is a fixed intrinsic property (e.g., cyanide is always toxic). Hazard is a contextual probability — it depends on dose, exposure route, and whether the substance is "not used in the prescribed manner and quantity." A substance can be toxic but pose negligible hazard if controlled (e.g., nitrite at legal ppm levels).

<Callout type="error" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP: Absolute Qualifier Alert">
A question may state *"Toxicity and Hazard are synonymous terms under Codex."* This is **FALSE**. Toxicity = capacity to cause harm (static). Hazard = probability of harm occurring given actual use conditions (dynamic/contextual). Never conflate the two on MCQ elimination logic.
</Callout>

### 1.3 Tripartite Classification of Hazards

**FOOD SAFETY HAZARDS**
* **BIOLOGICAL**
  * Macro-biological (visible)
  * Micro-biological (invisible: bacteria, yeast, mold, protozoa, viruses)
* **CHEMICAL**
  * Agri-chemicals (pesticides, hormones, antibiotics)
  * Pollution (heavy metals: Pb, Hg, As, Cd)
  * Food chemicals / naturally occurring toxicants
* **PHYSICAL**
  * Extraneous matter / foreign bodies

#### (A) Biological Hazards
**Intuition:** Micro-organisms are everywhere; they only become a "hazard" the moment they are (i) pathogenic to humans AND (ii) present in food in numbers/toxin-load sufficient to cause illness. Spoilage organisms ≠ pathogenic organisms — a food can look/smell fine and still carry a biological hazard (classic trap).
* **Macro-biological hazards:** visible pests and insects — larvae, adult insects, rodent droppings/hair — detectable by the naked eye.
* **Micro-biological hazards** (invisible without magnification):
  * Bacteria (e.g., *Salmonella*, *Clostridium botulinum*, *Staphylococcus aureus*)
  * Yeasts and moulds (spoilage + some mycotoxin producers)
  * Protozoa (parasitic)
  * Viruses (do not multiply in food, but survive and transmit via faecal-oral route)

#### (B) Chemical Hazards
| Sub-category | Examples | Entry Point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Agricultural chemicals** | Pesticides, fungicides, fertilizers, growth hormones, antibiotics | Primary production (crop/livestock) |
| **Environmental pollutants** | Heavy metals — Lead (Pb), Mercury (Hg), Arsenic (As), Cadmium (Cd) | Industrial discharge ➔ bioaccumulation along food chain, especially marine life |
| **Plant/equipment chemicals** | Cleaning agents, lubricants, sanitizer residues | Improper cleaning of food-contact surfaces |
| **Naturally occurring toxicants**| Mycotoxins (aflatoxins), glycoalkaloids, cyanogenic glycosides | Intrinsic to the food/crop (covered fully in Module 2) |
| **Food chemicals (additives)** | ~2,500+ chemicals used for preservation/flavour/appearance | Deliberately added, but hazard arises if controls fail |
| **Food allergens** | Protein-based — see Big 8 below | Natural constituents mislabelled/cross-contaminated |

> **Bioaccumulation mnemonic for heavy metals:** **"PHAC"** ➔ **P**b (Lead), **H**g (Mercury), **A**s (Arsenic), **C**d (Cadmium). These concentrate progressively up the food chain (biomagnification) — mercury in large predatory fish is the textbook case.

#### (C) Physical Hazards
Defined as any physical material not normally found in food, with potential to cause illness or injury.

| Material | Typical Source | Injury Potential |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Glass** | Bottles, jars, light fixtures, gauge covers | Cuts, bleeding, may need surgical removal |
| **Wood** | Fields, pallets, boxes, buildings | Cuts, infection, choking, may need surgery |
| **Stones** | Fields, buildings | Choking, broken teeth |
| **Metal** | Machinery, fields, wire, employees | Cuts, infection, may need surgery |
| **Insects** | Fields, plant, post-process entry | Illness, trauma, choking |

<Callout type="error" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP: Physical Hazards vs Defects">
A question may ask: *"Which of the following is NEVER a HACCP-relevant physical hazard: insects / glass / hair / metal fragments?"* Remember from Module 6: hair, filth, and simple insect presence without injury potential are classified as **"undesirable defects," NOT HACCP hazards**, unless they carry a direct injury/illness mechanism. Do not confuse general physical hazard taxonomy with the narrower HACCP hazard definition.
</Callout>

#### (D) Food Allergens — The "Big 8"
Statutory allergen list (protein-derived):
1. Milk
2. Egg
3. Fish
4. Crustacean shellfish
5. Tree nuts
6. Wheat
7. Peanuts
8. Soybeans

These are protein-derived hazards — the allergenic potential resides in specific proteins, not the whole food matrix. This is why highly refined oils carry much lower allergenic risk than the whole legume, though labelling precaution still applies.

### 1.4 Food Infection vs Food Intoxication — THE Definitive Distinction
**First-Principles Intuition:** Ask: *"Is the live organism itself the problem, or is a pre-formed poison the problem?"*
* **Infection** = you swallow a living army (pathogen) that invades and multiplies inside you. Damage accrues over hours–days. Think: slow siege.
* **Intoxication** = you swallow a poison that is already made (toxin), whether or not the organism that made it is still alive. Damage begins almost immediately. Think: instant poisoning.

| Parameter | Food Infection | Food Intoxication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cause** | Live pathogenic bacteria / viruses / parasites | Pre-formed toxin (bacterial, chemical, or natural) |
| **Mechanism** | Invasion and/or multiplication within intestinal lining or tissues | NO invasion or multiplication required; toxin acts directly |
| **Incubation period**| **Hours to days** (time needed to multiply) | **Minutes to hours** (toxin acts fast) |
| **Classic symptoms** | Diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, **fever** (sign of immune response) | Diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, double vision, respiratory failure, **fever typically absent** |
| **Transmissibility** | **CAN spread** person-to-person via faecal-oral route | **NOT communicable** (cannot "catch" a pre-formed toxin) |
| **Key factors** | Inadequate cooking, cross-contamination, poor personal hygiene | Time-temperature abuse allowing toxin formation prior to eating |
| **Classic examples** | *Salmonella* spp. (textbook food infection) | *Staphylococcus aureus* enterotoxin, *Clostridium botulinum* toxin |

<Callout type="error" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP: Fever & Transmissibility">
**"Fever" is a high-yield discriminator.** Infections typically present WITH fever; intoxications typically present WITHOUT fever but WITH neurological signs. If an MCQ gives a symptom set with double vision + respiratory failure + NO fever ➔ answer is intoxication (botulism). If it gives fever + cramps + multi-day onset ➔ answer is infection (*Salmonella*).
**Absolute qualifier:** *"Food intoxication is ALWAYS communicable."* — **FALSE.** Intoxication is not communicable person-to-person.
</Callout>

### 1.5 Food-Borne Disease: Classification & Transmission Routes
**Food-borne disease outbreak:** occurrence of 2 or more cases of similar illness from a common food source, OR observed cases exceeding the expected baseline number.

**Transmission Pathways (chain-of-custody view):**
* Infected food handler ➔ *faecal-oral / nasal flora* ➔ ready-to-eat food
* Cross-contamination ➔ *shared utensils/surfaces* ➔ uninfected food
* Inadequate cooking ➔ *survives heat-stable toxins* ➔ consumer
* Improper storage (10–50°C "danger zone") ➔ *pathogen multiplication* ➔ consumer

### 1.6 WHO Five Keys to Safer Food (Operational Framework)

> **KEY 1:** Keep Clean | **KEY 2:** Separate Raw & Cooked | **KEY 3:** Cook Thoroughly | **KEY 4:** Keep at Safe Temperatures | **KEY 5:** Use Safe Water & Raw Materials

| Key | Operational Guidance |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1. Keep clean** | Wash hands before handling food and often during preparation; sanitize all surfaces; protect kitchen areas from insects/pests. |
| **2. Separate raw & cooked** | Separate raw meat, poultry, and seafood from other foods; use separate equipment/utensils (cutting boards). |
| **3. Cook thoroughly** | Cook food thoroughly (meat, eggs, seafood); bring soups/stews to boiling to ensure pathogen destruction; reheat thoroughly. |
| **4. Keep food at safe temperatures** | Do not leave cooked food at room temp for >2 hours; refrigerate promptly (preferably <5°C); keep cooked food hot (>60°C); do not thaw at room temp. |
| **5. Use safe water & raw materials** | Use safe water; select fresh/wholesome foods; choose processed foods (e.g., pasteurized milk); wash raw fruits/vegetables; respect expiry dates. |

*(Mnemonic: C-S-C-T-W ➔ Clean, Separate, Cook, Temperature, Water)*

## MODULE 2: FOOD ADDITIVES & PLANT-ORIGIN TOXICANTS

### 2.1 First-Principles: Why classify additives?
Regulators do not ban "chemicals" per se — they distinguish between substances whose safety is self-evident from traditional use (Class I) versus substances that carry a dose-dependent risk profile requiring a legal ceiling (Class II). 

| Class I (Natural, unrestricted*) | Class II (Chemical, legally capped) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Common salt | Benzoic acid & its salts |
| Sugar, dextrose/glucose syrup | Sulphurous acid & its sulphites |
| Wood smoke, Spices | Nitrites & nitrates |
| Vinegar / acetic acid, Honey | Sorbic acid & its salts |
*\*unless otherwise provided in the Rules*

<Callout type="error" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP: Unrestricted Class I">
**Absolute qualifier:** *"Class I preservatives are permitted in UNLIMITED quantity in ALL foods."* — **FALSE**. Class I is "not restricted *unless otherwise provided in the Rules*" — a conditional, not an absolute, exemption.
</Callout>

### 2.2 Additive Categories — Mechanism Walkthrough

#### (A) Antioxidants
**Mechanism:** Fats/oils undergo autoxidation (free-radical chain reaction) converting unsaturated fatty acids into aldehydes/ketones (rancidity). Antioxidants donate a hydrogen atom to the lipid free radical, terminating the chain reaction.
* **Natural:** Tocopherols (Vitamin E). Heat-labile.
* **Synthetic:** BHA, BHT. BHA is highly effective for short-chain fatty acids.

#### (B) Emulsifiers & Stabilizers
**Mechanism:** Amphiphilic molecules (hydrophilic head + lipophilic tail) sit at the oil-water interface to stabilize two-phase systems.
* **HLB (Hydrophilic-Lipophilic Balance):** Determines whether it favors oil-in-water or water-in-oil emulsions.
* **Examples:** Monoglycerides (most widely used), Lecithin.

#### (C) Clarifying Agents
Used to remove chill haze (tannins/polyphenols) in beverages. Examples: Gelatin, PVP (polyvinylpyrrolidone).

#### (D) Colorants
* **Natural:** Carotenoid, annatto, anthocyanin, caramel, chlorophyll, turmeric.
* **Nature-Identical Synthetic:** Lab-synthesized replicas.
* **Artificial:** Coal-tar dyes (e.g., Indigocarmine).
* **Inorganic:** **Titanium Dioxide** (capped at 1% in CHEWING GUM only).

<Callout type="error" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP: Titanium Dioxide">
Titanium dioxide is permitted at **1% in chewing gum ONLY**. Any MCQ substituting a different percentage (e.g., 2%) or a different food (e.g., "all confectionery") is a trap.
</Callout>

#### (E) Flavour Enhancers & Sweeteners
* **MSG:** Enhances/synergizes flavours (umami).
* **5′-ribonucleotides:** ~10× the flavour-enhancing property of MSG, acts synergistically.
* **Non-nutritive sweeteners:** Saccharin (~300× sweeter than sucrose), aspartame, acesulfame-K.

#### (F) Anticaking Agents & Antibiotics
* **Anticaking:** Calcium silicate (table salt, up to 2%), Microcrystalline cellulose.
* **Antibiotics (Nisin):** Polypeptide antibiotic produced by streptococci. Prevents outgrowth of *Clostridium botulinum* in processed cheese. **Active against Gram-positive organisms only.**

### 2.4 Endogenous Plant Toxicants & Anti-Nutrients

#### (A) Glycoalkaloids — α-Chaconine & α-Solanine
* **Source:** Solanaceae plants (potatoes, tomatoes).
* **Limit:** 200 mg/kg (potatoes).
* **Detoxification:** **Thermally stable.** Baking/frying do NOT reduce content significantly. **Peeling** is the primary method (removes up to 60%). 
* *Note:* Tomatine in tomatoes degrades as the fruit ripens.

#### (B) Cyanogenic Glycosides
* **Mechanism:** Tissue damage brings the glycoside and endogenous plant enzyme (β-glycosidase) into contact ➔ Releases cyanohydrin ➔ Decomposes to **Hydrogen Cyanide (HCN)**.
* **Sources:** Linamarin (Cassava), Amygdalin (Stone fruit pits/almonds).
* **Detoxification:** Chopping, soaking in running water, fermentation, cooking (allows HCN to volatilize/leach out).

#### (C) Lectins (Haemagglutinins)
* **Mechanism:** Carbohydrate-binding glycoproteins. Survive GI digestion, bind to gut mucosal receptors causing damage.
* **Sources:** Legumes (Red kidney beans).
* **Detoxification:** Highly stable, requires vigorous boiling. Slow-cookers (sub-boiling) can actually increase toxicity.

#### (D) Anti-Nutrient Mnemonic Table
| Toxicant | Plant Family |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Glycoalkaloids** | Solanaceae (Potato/Tomato) |
| **Glucosinolates** | Cruciferae (Cabbage/Mustard) |
| **Cucurbitacins** | Cucurbitaceae (Gourds) |
| **Lectins / Proteinase Inhibitors** | Leguminosae (Beans/Legumes) |

## MODULE 3: MICROBIOLOGICAL PRINCIPLES & SPOILAGE CRITERIA

### 3.1 The Three-Lever Control Model
Every microorganism needs three things to grow: **Temperature**, **pH**, and **Water Activity ($a_w$)**.

#### 1. Cardinal Parameter: Temperature Domains
* **Psychrotrophs:** Grow well at ≤7°C (Optimum: 20–30°C)
* **Mesophiles:** Grow well at 20–45°C (Optimum: 30–40°C)
* **Thermophiles:** Grow well at ≥45°C (Optimum: 55–65°C)

#### 2. Cardinal Parameter: pH Thresholds
* **Most organisms:** near pH 7.0
* **Lactic acid bacteria:** can grow as low as pH 2.0
* **Clostridium botulinum:** **Cannot grow below pH 4.6**

> **THE Critical Public Health Threshold:** pH 4.6 is the universal cutoff differentiating Low-acid foods (pH > 4.6, require full retort sterilization) from Acid foods (pH ≤ 4.6, safer from *C. botulinum*).

#### 3. Cardinal Parameter: Water Activity ($a_w$)
* Fresh produce: $a_w > 0.99$
* Spoilage bacteria: $a_w > 0.91$
* Filamentous fungi / moulds: can grow at $a_w$ as low as **0.80**

### 3.5 Spoilage Comparison Summary
| Organism Group | Competitive Niche | Key Genera |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bacteria** | Neutral pH, moderate-high $a_w$ | Soft rot bacteria |
| **Yeasts** | High sugar/salt, low pH | *Zygosaccharomyces*, *Candida* |
| **Filamentous fungi** | Low $a_w$ (down to 0.80), wide pH tolerance | Various moulds (Obligate aerobes) |

## MODULE 4: POST-HARVEST ADULTERATION

### 4.2 Artificial Ripening Agents
**Calcium Carbide ("Masala") — STRICTLY BANNED**
* **Mechanism:** $\text{CaC}_2 + \text{Moisture} \rightarrow \text{Acetylene gas (C}_2\text{H}_2\text{)}$. Acetylene mimics ethylene superficially.
* **Why Banned:** Trace contamination with toxic **arsenic and phosphorus hydride**. Produces superficial colouration without internal ripening.
* **Safe Alternative:** Ethephon / Ethylene Gas.

### 4.3 Hazardous Dyes & Chemical Malpractices
| Chemical | Commodity Target | Toxic Effect / Detection |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Malachite Green** | Bitter gourd, green chili | Carcinogenic; **Blotting Paper Test** |
| **Copper sulfate** | Lady fingers | Anaemia, abdominal pain |
| **Methanil yellow / Lead chromate** | Watermelons | Neurotoxic, reproductive degeneration |
| **Oxytocin** | Cucurbits/gourds | Mammalian hormone misused for size enlargement |

### 4.4 Food-Grade Waxing Regulations
* **Approved Waxes:** Carnauba wax, Beeswax, Shellac wax.
* **NOT Allowed:** Mineral oils or unapproved petroleum waxes.
* **Labelling:** Waxed produce MUST have a clear label declaration stating the origin of the wax.

### 5.2 Statutory Date Markings
* **"Best before":** Signifies the end of the period for peak quality. Food MAY still be perfectly safe to consume post-date unless independently unsafe.
* **"Use by / Expiry date":** Absolute safety cutoff. Food SHALL NOT be sold after this date.

## MODULE 6: THE HACCP SYSTEM & QUALITY ASSURANCE

### 6.1 First-Principles
HACCP shifts control upstream from end-product testing to real-time process monitoring.
* **Origin:** Pillsbury Company (1960s) for NASA.
* **Formalization:** NACMCF (1985).

### 6.3 Hazard vs Undesirable Defect
In HACCP, "hazard" means potential to cause illness/injury.
* **Undesirable Defects:** Hair, filth, spoilage, economic fraud, insect presence (without direct injury potential) are NOT HACCP hazards.

### 6.4 The 7 Core HACCP Principles
> **1. Conduct Hazard Analysis** ➔ **2. Determine CCPs** ➔ **3. Establish Critical Limits** ➔ **4. Establish Monitoring Procedures** ➔ **5. Establish Corrective Actions** ➔ **6. Establish Verification Procedures** ➔ **7. Establish Documentation & Record-Keeping**

### 6.5 CCP vs Prerequisite Programme (PRP)
| Feature | CCP (Critical Control Point) | PRP (Prerequisite Programme) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scope** | Specific point directly controlling a significant hazard | General, plant-wide foundational practices (GMP, GHP, sanitation) |
| **Monitoring** | Continuous/frequent, tied to a measurable limit | Periodic inspection/audit |
| **Examples** | Pasteurization temp/time; Metal detector | Water potability; pest control |

<Callout type="error" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP: CCP Decision Logic">
**Absolute qualifier:** *"Every step that controls a hazard is automatically a CCP."* — **FALSE**. If a LATER step in the process will adequately eliminate/reduce the SAME hazard to an acceptable level, the EARLIER step is not designated a CCP.
</Callout>

## 6.6 Stage 2 Model Answer Framework (15 Marks)
**Question: Elaborate the 7 Principles of Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point (HACCP). Explain how a Critical Control Point (CCP) is determined and distinguished from a Prerequisite Programme (PRP).**

### 1. Introduction & Historical Foundations (1 mark)
HACCP is a proactive, preventative food safety management system designed to identify and control hazards rather than relying on retrospective finished product testing. Pioneered in the early 1960s by the Pillsbury Company alongside NASA to ensure absolute food safety for manned space flights, the system was formally codified into 7 core principles by the National Advisory Committee on Microbiological Criteria for Foods (NACMCF) in 1985[cite: 22].

### 2. The 7 Core HACCP Principles (9 marks)
* **Principle 1: Conduct Hazard Analysis:** The HACCP team identifies and assesses all potential biological, chemical, and physical hazards capable of causing consumer illness or injury[cite: 22]. Crucially, safety concerns are separated from quality defects (such as hair or filth, which do not pose direct health hazards)[cite: 22].
* **Principle 2: Determine Critical Control Points (CCPs):** A CCP is a specific point or step in a food process where control can be applied to prevent, eliminate, or reduce a food safety hazard to an acceptable level[cite: 22]. CCPs are systematically determined using structured **Decision Tree logic**[cite: 22].
* **Principle 3: Establish Critical Limits:** For each CCP, measurable boundaries (criteria that must be met) are defined to differentiate between safe and unsafe operating conditions (e.g., thermal boundaries, time, pH, water activity)[cite: 22].
* **Principle 4: Establish Monitoring Procedures:** A scheduled sequence of observations or measurements is established to ensure the CCP remains within critical limits[cite: 22]. Effective monitoring protocols address the four fundamentals: **What** is monitored, **How** it is monitored, **Frequency**, and **Who** carries out the monitoring[cite: 22].
* **Principle 5: Establish Corrective Actions:** Predetermined, documented protocols executed immediately when monitoring indicates a deviation from an established critical limit[cite: 22]. They must: (1) restore the process to control, and (2) segregate, evaluate, and determine the safe disposition of affected product[cite: 22].
* **Principle 6: Establish Verification Procedures:** The application of methods, procedures, tests, and other evaluations to confirm compliance with the HACCP plan[cite: 22]. This incorporates **Validation** (scientific substantiation conducted upfront prior to implementation) and ongoing **Verification** (audits, calibrations, review of monitoring logs)[cite: 22].
* **Principle 7: Establish Documentation and Record-Keeping:** Maintenance of complete records demonstrating safe manufacture[cite: 22]. This includes support documentation for critical limits, CCP monitoring logs, corrective action reports, and verification/audit results[cite: 22].

### 3. Determination of a CCP via Decision Tree (3 marks)
A process step is designated as a CCP by asking a sequence of logical questions[cite: 22]:
1. Does a control measure exist at this step for the identified hazard[cite: 22]?
2. Is control at this step necessary for safety[cite: 22]?
3. Is the hazard likely to occur above acceptable levels or increase to an unacceptable level at this step[cite: 22]?
4. **Will a subsequent step eliminate or reduce the hazard to an acceptable level[cite: 22]?** 
   * *If YES* ➔ The step is **NOT a CCP** (the hazard will be eliminated downstream)[cite: 22].
   * *If NO* ➔ **THIS STEP IS A CCP**[cite: 22].

### 4. CCP vs Prerequisite Programme (PRP) Distinction (2 marks)

| Parameter | Critical Control Point (CCP) | Prerequisite Programme (PRP) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scope** | Point-specific and hazard-specific (e.g., pasteurization holding tube)[cite: 22]. | Broad, plant-wide foundational hygiene (e.g., GMP, GHP, pest control, water potability)[cite: 22]. |
| **Derivation** | Derived through hazard analysis and Decision Tree logic[cite: 22]. | Universal baseline requirements independent of specific product flows[cite: 22]. |
| **Criteria** | Controlled by strict, measurable numerical Critical Limits[cite: 22]. | Monitored through general audits, inspection checklists, and SOP compliance[cite: 22]. |
| **Deviation Consequence** | Poses immediate direct food safety hazard; mandates immediate product isolation and corrective disposition[cite: 22]. | Addressed via routine facility maintenance and corrective sanitation without necessarily halting batches[cite: 22]. |
