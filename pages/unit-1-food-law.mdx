## MODULE 1: FOOD HAZARDS & FOODBORNE ILLNESSES

### 1.1 First-Principles: Why does this module exist?
Before memorising definitions, build the mental model[span_2](start_span)[span_2](end_span). Every food safety question ultimately reduces to one chain[span_3](start_span)[span_3](end_span):

<Flow>
  **SOURCE** (raw material / environment / handler) 
  ↓ 
  **HAZARD** enters food (biological / chemical / physical) 
  ↓ 
  **HAZARD** survives processing / storage 
  ↓ 
  **HAZARD** reaches consumer in a dose sufficient to harm 
  ↓ 
  **ADVERSE HEALTH EFFECT** (illness, injury, chronic disease)
</Flow>

Food safety science is simply the discipline of breaking this chain at the earliest possible point[span_4](start_span)[span_4](end_span). Everything in this module — definitions, hazard classes, infection vs intoxication, the WHO Five Keys — is organised around where in this chain you intervene[span_5](start_span)[span_5](end_span).

### 1.2 Codified Definitions (verbatim-grade, exam-ready)

| Term | Definition (exam-ready) | Conceptual Anchor |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Food Safety** | Assurance that food will not cause harm to the consumer when prepared and/or eaten according to its intended use[span_6](start_span)[span_6](end_span). | Outcome-based guarantee[span_7](start_span)[span_7](end_span) |
| **Toxicity** | The capacity of a substance to produce harm or injury of any kind, under any condition of exposure[span_8](start_span)[span_8](end_span). | Intrinsic property of the substance[span_9](start_span)[span_9](end_span) |
| **Hazard (Codex Alimentarius)** | "A biological, chemical or physical agent in, or condition of, food with the potential to cause an adverse health effect.[span_10](start_span)"[span_10](end_span) | Potential, not certainty[span_11](start_span)[span_11](end_span) |
| **Contamination** | Presence of harmful or objectionable foreign substances (chemicals, micro-organisms, dilutants) in food, before/during/after processing or storage[span_12](start_span)[span_12](end_span). | Unintended presence[span_13](start_span)[span_13](end_span) |
| **Adulteration** | Intentional or accidental addition of impure, cheap, or unnecessary ingredients to cheat, cheapen, or falsify a preparation, altering composition/quality[span_14](start_span)[span_14](end_span). | Deliberate or negligent degradation[span_15](start_span)[span_15](end_span) |
| **Food Quality** | Attributes that influence a product's value to the consumer — negative (spoilage, contamination, adulteration, safety hazards) AND positive (colour, flavour, texture)[span_16](start_span)[span_16](end_span). | Quality ⊃ Safety (quality is the wider umbrella)[span_17](start_span)[span_17](end_span) |

**Toxicity vs Hazard** — the single most tested distinction[span_18](start_span)[span_18](end_span). Toxicity is a fixed intrinsic property (e.g., cyanide is always toxic)[span_19](start_span)[span_19](end_span). Hazard is a contextual probability — it depends on dose, exposure route, and whether the substance is "not used in the prescribed manner and quantity.[span_20](start_span)"[span_20](end_span) A substance can be toxic but pose negligible hazard if controlled (e.g., nitrite at legal ppm levels)[span_21](start_span)[span_21](end_span).

<Callout type="danger" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP: Absolute qualifier alert">
A question may state *"Toxicity and Hazard are synonymous terms under Codex."* This is **FALSE**[span_22](start_span)[span_22](end_span). Toxicity = capacity to cause harm (static)[span_23](start_span)[span_23](end_span). Hazard = probability of harm occurring given actual use conditions (dynamic/contextual)[span_24](start_span)[span_24](end_span). Never conflate the two on MCQ elimination logic[span_25](start_span)[span_25](end_span).
</Callout>

### 1.3 Tripartite Classification of Hazards

**FOOD SAFETY HAZARDS**[span_26](start_span)[span_26](end_span)
* **BIOLOGICAL**[span_27](start_span)[span_27](end_span)
  * Macro-biological (visible)[span_28](start_span)[span_28](end_span)
  * Micro-biological (invisible: bacteria, yeast, mold, protozoa, viruses)[span_29](start_span)[span_29](end_span)
* **CHEMICAL**[span_30](start_span)[span_30](end_span)
  * Agri-chemicals (pesticides, hormones, antibiotics)[span_31](start_span)[span_31](end_span)
  * Pollution (heavy metals: Pb, Hg, As, Cd)[span_32](start_span)[span_32](end_span)
  * Food chemicals / naturally occurring toxicants[span_33](start_span)[span_33](end_span)
* **PHYSICAL**[span_34](start_span)[span_34](end_span)
  * Extraneous matter / foreign bodies[span_35](start_span)[span_35](end_span)

#### (A) Biological Hazards
**Intuition:** Micro-organisms are everywhere; they only become a "hazard" the moment they are (i) pathogenic to humans AND (ii) present in food in numbers/toxin-load sufficient to cause illness[span_36](start_span)[span_36](end_span). Spoilage organisms ≠ pathogenic organisms — a food can look/smell fine and still carry a biological hazard (classic trap)[span_37](start_span)[span_37](end_span).

* **Macro-biological hazards:** visible pests and insects — larvae, adult insects, rodent droppings/hair — detectable by the naked eye[span_38](start_span)[span_38](end_span).
* **Micro-biological hazards (invisible without magnification):**
  * Bacteria (e.g., *Salmonella*, *Clostridium botulinum*, *Staphylococcus aureus*)[span_39](start_span)[span_39](end_span)
  * Yeasts and moulds (spoilage + some mycotoxin producers)[span_40](start_span)[span_40](end_span)
  * Protozoa (parasitic)[span_41](start_span)[span_41](end_span)
  * Viruses (do not multiply in food, but survive and transmit via faecal-oral route)[span_42](start_span)[span_42](end_span)

#### (B) Chemical Hazards
| Sub-category | Examples | Entry Point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Agricultural chemicals** | Pesticides, fungicides, fertilizers, growth hormones, antibiotics[span_43](start_span)[span_43](end_span) | Primary production (crop/livestock)[span_44](start_span)[span_44](end_span) |
| **Environmental pollutants** | Heavy metals — Lead (Pb), Mercury (Hg), Arsenic (As), Cadmium (Cd)[span_45](start_span)[span_45](end_span) | Industrial discharge → bioaccumulation along food chain, especially marine life[span_46](start_span)[span_46](end_span) |
| **Plant/equipment chemicals** | Cleaning agents, lubricants, sanitizer residues[span_47](start_span)[span_47](end_span) | Improper cleaning of food-contact surfaces[span_48](start_span)[span_48](end_span) |
| **Naturally occurring toxicants** | Mycotoxins (aflatoxins), glycoalkaloids, cyanogenic glycosides[span_49](start_span)[span_49](end_span) | Intrinsic to the food/crop (covered fully in Module 2)[span_50](start_span)[span_50](end_span) |
| **Food chemicals (additives)** | ~2,500+ chemicals used for preservation/flavour/appearance[span_51](start_span)[span_51](end_span) | Deliberately added, but hazard arises if controls fail[span_52](start_span)[span_52](end_span) |
| **Food allergens** | Protein-based — see Big 8 below[span_53](start_span)[span_53](end_span) | Natural constituents mislabelled/cross-contaminated[span_54](start_span)[span_54](end_span) |

**Bioaccumulation mnemonic for heavy metals:** **"PHAC"** → **P**b (Lead), **H**g (Mercury), **A**s (Arsenic), **C**d (Cadmium)[span_55](start_span)[span_55](end_span). These concentrate progressively up the food chain (biomagnification) — mercury in large predatory fish is the textbook case[span_56](start_span)[span_56](end_span).

#### (C) Physical Hazards
Defined as any physical material not normally found in food, with potential to cause illness or injury[span_57](start_span)[span_57](end_span).

| Material | Typical Source | Injury Potential |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Glass** | Bottles, jars, light fixtures, gauge covers[span_58](start_span)[span_58](end_span) | Cuts, bleeding, may need surgical removal[span_59](start_span)[span_59](end_span) |
| **Wood** | Fields, pallets, boxes, buildings[span_60](start_span)[span_60](end_span) | Cuts, infection, choking, may need surgery[span_61](start_span)[span_61](end_span) |
| **Stones** | Fields, buildings[span_62](start_span)[span_62](end_span) | Choking, broken teeth[span_63](start_span)[span_63](end_span) |
| **Metal** | Machinery, fields, wire, employees[span_64](start_span)[span_64](end_span) | Cuts, infection, may need surgery[span_65](start_span)[span_65](end_span) |
| **Insects** | Fields, plant, post-process entry[span_66](start_span)[span_66](end_span) | Illness, trauma, choking[span_67](start_span)[span_67](end_span) |

<Callout type="danger" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP">
A question may ask: *"Which of the following is NEVER a HACCP-relevant physical hazard: insects / glass / hair / metal fragments?"* Remember from Module 6: hair, filth, and simple insect presence without injury potential are classified as "undesirable defects," NOT HACCP hazards, unless they carry a direct injury/illness mechanism[span_68](start_span)[span_68](end_span). Do not confuse general physical hazard taxonomy (this module) with the narrower HACCP hazard definition (Module 6)[span_69](start_span)[span_69](end_span).
</Callout>

#### (D) Food Allergens — The "Big 8"
Statutory allergen list (protein-derived):
1. Milk[span_70](start_span)[span_70](end_span)
2. Egg[span_71](start_span)[span_71](end_span)
3. Fish[span_72](start_span)[span_72](end_span)
4. Crustacean shellfish[span_73](start_span)[span_73](end_span)
5. Tree nuts[span_74](start_span)[span_74](end_span)
6. Wheat[span_75](start_span)[span_75](end_span)
7. Peanuts[span_76](start_span)[span_76](end_span)
8. Soybeans[span_77](start_span)[span_77](end_span)

These are protein-derived hazards — the allergenic potential resides in specific proteins, not the whole food matrix[span_78](start_span)[span_78](end_span). This is why highly refined oils (e.g., refined soybean/peanut oil, protein largely removed) carry much lower allergenic risk than the whole legume, though labelling precaution still applies[span_79](start_span)[span_79](end_span).

### 1.4 Food Infection vs Food Intoxication — THE definitive distinction
**First-Principles Intuition**
Ask: *"Is the live organism itself the problem, or is a pre-formed poison the problem?"*[span_80](start_span)[span_80](end_span)
* **Infection** = you swallow a living army (pathogen) that invades and multiplies inside you[span_81](start_span)[span_81](end_span). Damage accrues over hours–days as the population builds up[span_82](start_span)[span_82](end_span). Think: slow siege[span_83](start_span)[span_83](end_span).
* **Intoxication** = you swallow a poison that is already made (toxin), whether or not the organism that made it is still alive[span_84](start_span)[span_84](end_span). Damage can begin almost immediately[span_85](start_span)[span_85](end_span). Think: instant poisoning[span_86](start_span)[span_86](end_span).

**Master Comparison Table**
| Parameter | Food Infection | Food Intoxication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cause** | Live pathogenic bacteria / viruses / parasites[span_87](start_span)[span_87](end_span) | Pre-formed toxin (bacterial, chemical, or natural)[span_88](start_span)[span_88](end_span) |
| **Mechanism** | Invasion and/or multiplication within intestinal lining or tissues (may also release toxin after invasion)[span_89](start_span)[span_89](end_span) | NO invasion or multiplication required; toxin acts directly[span_90](start_span)[span_90](end_span) |
| **Incubation period** | Hours to days (time needed for organism to multiply to infective dose)[span_91](start_span)[span_91](end_span) | Minutes to hours (toxin already present, acts fast)[span_92](start_span)[span_92](end_span) |
| **Classic symptoms** | Diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, **fever** (sign of active infection/immune response)[span_93](start_span)[span_93](end_span) | Diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, double vision, weakness, respiratory failure, numbness, sensory/motor dysfunction (neurotoxic patterns), **fever typically absent**[span_94](start_span)[span_94](end_span) |
| **Transmissibility** | CAN spread person-to-person via faecal-oral route[span_95](start_span)[span_95](end_span) | NOT communicable (you cannot "catch" someone else's pre-formed toxin exposure)[span_96](start_span)[span_96](end_span) |
| **Key contributing factors** | Inadequate cooking, cross-contamination, poor personal hygiene, bare-hand contact[span_97](start_span)[span_97](end_span) | Inadequate cooking/time-temperature abuse allowing toxin formation, improper holding temperatures[span_98](start_span)[span_98](end_span) |
| **Classic examples** | *Salmonella* spp. (textbook food infection)[span_99](start_span)[span_99](end_span) | *Staphylococcus aureus* enterotoxin, *Clostridium botulinum* toxin, *Bacillus cereus* toxin[span_100](start_span)[span_100](end_span) |

<Callout type="danger" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP">
"Fever" is a high-yield discriminator[span_101](start_span)[span_101](end_span). Infections more typically present WITH fever (active immune battle against live organisms); intoxications more typically present WITHOUT fever (no active infection, pure toxin effect) but WITH neurological signs[span_102](start_span)[span_102](end_span). If an MCQ gives a symptom set with double vision + respiratory failure + NO fever → answer is intoxication (think botulism)[span_103](start_span)[span_103](end_span). If it gives fever + cramps + multi-day onset → answer is infection (think *Salmonella*)[span_104](start_span)[span_104](end_span).

**Absolute qualifier:** *"Food intoxication is ALWAYS communicable."* — FALSE[span_105](start_span)[span_105](end_span). By definition, intoxication is not communicable person-to-person; only infections carry the faecal-oral transmission risk[span_106](start_span)[span_106](end_span).
</Callout>

### 1.5 Food-Borne Disease: Classification & Transmission Routes
**Food-borne disease outbreak (definition):** occurrence of 2 or more cases of similar illness from a common food source, OR observed cases exceeding the expected baseline number for that illness[span_107](start_span)[span_107](end_span).

**Transmission Pathways (chain-of-custody view)**[span_108](start_span)[span_108](end_span)
* Infected food handler ─faecal-oral / nasal flora─► ready-to-eat food[span_109](start_span)[span_109](end_span)
* Cross-contamination ─shared utensils/surfaces─► uninfected food[span_110](start_span)[span_110](end_span)
* Inadequate cooking ─survives heat-stable toxins─► consumer[span_111](start_span)[span_111](end_span)
* Improper storage (10–50°C "danger zone") ─pathogen multiplication─► consumer[span_112](start_span)[span_112](end_span)

| Route | Mechanism | Prevention Point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Infected individuals** | Pathogens shed in faeces; transferred via faecal-oral route; infected skin lesions/nasal flora transferred to ready-to-eat food[span_113](start_span)[span_113](end_span) | Personnel health screening, hygiene[span_114](start_span)[span_114](end_span) |
| **Cross-contamination** | Same equipment/utensils used across raw and ready-to-eat food without washing/disinfecting[span_115](start_span)[span_115](end_span) | Separate equipment, cleaning SOPs[span_116](start_span)[span_116](end_span) |
| **Inadequate cooking** | Insufficient heat allows multiplication; many bacterial toxins are heat-stable and survive cooking[span_117](start_span)[span_117](end_span) | Validated cook temperatures[span_118](start_span)[span_118](end_span) |
| **Improper storage** | Holding food at 10–50°C (danger zone) permits pathogen multiplication[span_119](start_span)[span_119](end_span) | Time-temperature control[span_120](start_span)[span_120](end_span) |

### 1.6 WHO Five Keys to Safer Food (complete operational framework)

<Flow>
  **KEY 1:** Keep Clean | 
  **KEY 2:** Separate Raw & Cooked | 
  **KEY 3:** Cook Thoroughly | 
  **KEY 4:** Keep at Safe Temperatures | 
  **KEY 5:** Use Safe Water & Raw Materials
</Flow>

| Key | Operational Guidance |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1. Keep clean** | Wash hands before handling food and often during preparation; wash/sanitize all surfaces and equipment used for food preparation; protect kitchen areas and food from insects, pests, and other animals[span_121](start_span)[span_121](end_span). |
| **2. Separate raw and cooked** | Separate raw meat, poultry, and seafood from other foods; use separate equipment and utensils (cutting boards, knives) for handling raw foods; store food in containers to avoid contact between raw and prepared foods[span_122](start_span)[span_122](end_span). |
| **3. Cook thoroughly** | Cook food thoroughly, especially meat, poultry, eggs, and seafood; bring foods like soups and stews to boiling to ensure pathogen destruction; reheat cooked food thoroughly[span_123](start_span)[span_123](end_span). |
| **4. Keep food at safe temperatures** | Do not leave cooked food at room temperature for more than 2 hours; refrigerate promptly (preferably below 5°C); keep cooked food hot (above 60°C) prior to serving; do not store food too long even in the refrigerator; do not thaw frozen food at room temperature[span_124](start_span)[span_124](end_span). |
| **5. Use safe water and raw materials** | Use safe water or treat it to make it safe; select fresh and wholesome foods; choose foods processed for safety (e.g., pasteurized milk); wash fruits and vegetables, especially if eaten raw; do not use food beyond its expiry date[span_125](start_span)[span_125](end_span). |

Mnemonic: **C-S-C-T-W** → Clean, Separate, Cook, Temperature, Water[span_126](start_span)[span_126](end_span).

## MODULE 2: FOOD ADDITIVES & PLANT-ORIGIN TOXICANTS

### 2.1 First-Principles: Why classify additives at all?
Regulators do not ban "chemicals" per se — they distinguish between substances whose safety is self-evident from long traditional use and low toxicity even unrestricted (Class I) versus substances that are effective but carry a dose-dependent risk profile requiring a legal ceiling (Class II)[span_150](start_span)[span_150](end_span). This single idea — unrestricted vs quantity-capped — is the organizing logic behind nearly every additive question HPSC asks[span_151](start_span)[span_151](end_span).

**FOOD PRESERVATIVES**[span_152](start_span)[span_152](end_span)
* **CLASS I (natural, NOT restricted in any food unless Rules say otherwise)**[span_153](start_span)[span_153](end_span)
  * Common salt, sugar, dextrose/glucose syrup, wood smoke, spices, vinegar/acetic acid, honey[span_154](start_span)[span_154](end_span)
* **CLASS II (chemical, RESTRICTED to specified foods, concentration capped per Rules)**[span_155](start_span)[span_155](end_span)
  * Benzoic acid & salts, Sulphurous acid & sulphites, Nitrites & nitrates, Sorbic acid & salts[span_156](start_span)[span_156](end_span)

<Callout type="danger" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP">
**Absolute qualifier:** *"Class I preservatives are permitted in UNLIMITED quantity in ALL foods."* — Partially FALSE[span_157](start_span)[span_157](end_span). Class I is "not restricted unless otherwise provided in the Rules" — a conditional, not an absolute, exemption[span_158](start_span)[span_158](end_span). Always flag "unlimited/unrestricted/all" wording against the actual legal phrasing[span_159](start_span)[span_159](end_span).
</Callout>

### 2.2 Additive Categories — Mechanism-First Walkthrough

#### (a) Antioxidants
**Intuition:** Fats and oils undergo autoxidation — a free-radical chain reaction with atmospheric oxygen that converts unsaturated fatty acids into aldehydes/ketones, producing rancid off-flavours, loss of colour, and destruction of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E) and essential fatty acids (e.g., linoleic acid)[span_160](start_span)[span_160](end_span).

Unsaturated fatty acid + O2 (catalyzed by light, heat, metal, pigment) 
↓ 
Lipid free radical (R•) 
↓ (chain propagation) 
Peroxide → Aldehydes + Ketones → RANCID OFF-FLAVOUR[span_161](start_span)[span_161](end_span)

An antioxidant works by donating a hydrogen atom to the lipid free radical, terminating the chain reaction before it propagates (free radical scavenging)[span_162](start_span)[span_162](end_span).

| Type | Examples | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Natural** | Tocopherols (Vitamin E family)[span_163](start_span)[span_163](end_span) | Found in plant tissues; heat-labile — lost during refining, deodorizing, processing[span_164](start_span)[span_164](end_span) |
| **Synthetic** | BHA, BHT[span_165](start_span)[span_165](end_span) | BHA is more effective in animal fats than vegetable oils; very effective for short-chain fatty acids (coconut, palm kernel oil) used in cereals/confectionery[span_166](start_span)[span_166](end_span) |

#### (b) Emulsifiers & Stabilizers
**Intuition:** Oil and water naturally separate because they are chemically incompatible (one polar, one non-polar)[span_167](start_span)[span_167](end_span). An emulsifier is amphiphilic — it has both a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and a lipophilic (fat-loving) tail — allowing it to sit at the oil-water interface and stabilize what is naturally an unstable two-phase system[span_168](start_span)[span_168](end_span).

**HLB (Hydrophilic-Lipophilic Balance):** a numeric scale describing the relative strength of hydrophilic vs lipophilic portions of an emulsifier molecule; determines whether the emulsifier favours oil-in-water or water-in-oil emulsions[span_169](start_span)[span_169](end_span).

Most widely used emulsifier: monoglycerides; lecithin is another key natural emulsifier[span_170](start_span)[span_170](end_span).

#### (c) Clarifying Agents
Used to remove chill haze (from tannins/proanthocyanidins/polyphenols) in beverages like beer, wine, fruit juices[span_171](start_span)[span_171](end_span).
* **Gelatin** — commonly used to clarify beverages[span_172](start_span)[span_172](end_span).
* **PVP (polyvinylpyrrolidone)** — synthetic resin with selective affinity for polyphenols[span_173](start_span)[span_173](end_span).

#### (d) Colorants
**FOOD COLORANTS**[span_174](start_span)[span_174](end_span)

* **NATURE-IDENTICAL SYNTHETIC:** (lab-synthesized replicas of natural colors)[span_176](start_span)[span_176](end_span)

#### (e) Flavour Enhancers & Sweeteners
* **MSG (Monosodium Glutamate):** sodium salt of glutamic acid; does not itself possess a primary flavour but intensifies/synergizes flavours of other foods[span_183](start_span)[span_183](end_span).
* **5′-ribonucleotides (from yeast):** possess roughly 10× the flavour-enhancing property of MSG, and act synergistically with it (umami synergy)[span_184](start_span)[span_184](end_span).
* **Non-nutritive sweeteners:** Saccharin (~300× sweeter than sucrose), cyclamate, aspartame, acesulfame-K — none perfectly replicates full sucrose taste profile[span_185](start_span)[span_185](end_span).

#### (f) Anticaking Agents
**Function:** absorb excess moisture, coat particles for water repellency, or act as an insoluble particulate diluent to maintain free-flow of hygroscopic powders[span_186](start_span)[span_186](end_span).
* **Calcium silicate:** used in baking powder and table salt, up to 2%[span_187](start_span)[span_187](end_span).
* **Microcrystalline cellulose:** prevents grated/shredded cheese from clumping[span_188](start_span)[span_188](end_span).
* **Other agents:** sodium silicon aluminate, tri-calcium phosphate, magnesium silicate, magnesium carbonate[span_189](start_span)[span_189](end_span).

#### (g) Antibiotics in Food — Nisin
* **Nisin:** a polypeptide antibiotic produced by streptococci[span_190](start_span)[span_190](end_span).
* **Permitted use:** prevents outgrowth of *Clostridium botulinum* in high-moisture processed cheese[span_191](start_span)[span_191](end_span).
* **Spectrum:** active against Gram-positive organisms (spore-formers in particular)[span_192](start_span)[span_192](end_span).
* Also used to prevent spoilage of condensed milk[span_193](start_span)[span_193](end_span).
* Nisin's narrow Gram-positive spectrum is the key testable nuance — it does NOT control Gram-negative spoilage/pathogenic organisms[span_194](start_span)[span_194](end_span).

#### (h) Nitrites & Nitrates
**Function:** stabilize colour of cured meat after cooking (prevent green-brown discoloration of lean meat), impart flavour, and critically act as inhibitors of *Clostridium botulinum* (anti-botulism preservative function)[span_195](start_span)[span_195](end_span).
* Lethal dose for humans is of the order of 1 gram[span_196](start_span)[span_196](end_span).
* US Code of Federal Regulations cap: nitrites permitted as preservatives in cured meat/poultry not exceeding 2000 ppm[span_197](start_span)[span_197](end_span).

### 2.4 Endogenous Plant Toxicants & Anti-Nutrients — Deep Mechanistic Treatment

#### (a) Glycoalkaloids — α-Chaconine & α-Solanine
**First principles:** These are steroidal glycoalkaloids produced by Solanaceae plants (potato, tomato, nightshade) as a natural chemical defence against pests[span_209](start_span)[span_209](end_span). They act on humans via cholinesterase inhibition and cell membrane disruption (saponin-like action)[span_210](start_span)[span_210](end_span).

| Parameter | Detail |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Source** | All Solanaceous plants — potatoes most significant staple exposure[span_211](start_span)[span_211](end_span) |
| **Key compounds** | α-chaconine + α-solanine (summed)[span_212](start_span)[span_212](end_span) |
| **Maximum safety limit** | 200 mg/kg (potatoes), based on an estimated 300 g/day intake[span_213](start_span)[span_213](end_span) |
| **Variability factors** | Variety, light exposure, irradiation, mechanical injury, storage conditions[span_214](start_span)[span_214](end_span) |
| **Thermal stability** | Largely unaffected by common processing — baking, cooking, frying do NOT significantly reduce glycoalkaloid content[span_215](start_span)[span_215](end_span) |
| **Detoxification** | Peeling is the most efficient method — outer layers are richest in toxin; manual peeling can remove up to 60% of total content (varies with tuber size/variety)[span_216](start_span)[span_216](end_span) |
| **Tomato-specific note** | Tomatine (α-tomatine + dehydrotomatine mixture) is largely degraded as the fruit ripens — tomatoes are therefore much safer than potatoes regarding glycoalkaloid exposure[span_217](start_span)[span_217](end_span) |

<Callout type="danger" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP">
**Absolute qualifier:** *"Cooking/boiling/frying completely detoxifies glycoalkaloids."* — FALSE[span_218](start_span)[span_218](end_span). Glycoalkaloids are thermally stable; peeling (not heat) is the primary mitigation, and even peeling removes only up to ~60%, not 100%[span_219](start_span)[span_219](end_span).
</Callout>

**Traditional detoxification practices** (cassava, Africa/South America): chopping, grinding, soaking in running water, fermentation, cooking — these physically disrupt plant tissue, allow HCN to volatilize/leach out, and denature enzymes[span_225](start_span)[span_225](end_span).
Amygdalin risk specifically arises when heat-processed products made from stone fruits retain the seeds/pits — consumers may be unknowingly exposed to HCN from unremoved seeds[span_226](start_span)[span_226](end_span).

#### (c) Lectins (Haemagglutinins)
* **Chemical nature:** carbohydrate-binding glycoproteins, ubiquitous in nature[span_227](start_span)[span_227](end_span).
* **Dietary relevance:** present in most commonly eaten plant foods, especially legumes — consumed daily in appreciable quantities[span_228](start_span)[span_228](end_span).
* **Key toxicological feature:** high stability — survive gastrointestinal digestion, binding to gut mucosal carbohydrate receptors and causing gastric upset / mucosal damage via disruption of nutrient absorption and gut lining integrity[span_229](start_span)[span_229](end_span).

#### (d) Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids
* Large group — ~350 distinct chemicals[span_230](start_span)[span_230](end_span).
* **Toxicological classification:** shown carcinogenic in animals; classified as potential human carcinogens (hepatotoxic mechanism — liver injury)[span_231](start_span)[span_231](end_span).
* **Exposure routes:** (i) contamination of food supply by seeds containing these alkaloids, (ii) presence in traditional remedies/dietary supplements[span_232](start_span)[span_232](end_span).

## MODULE 3: MICROBIOLOGICAL PRINCIPLES & SPOILAGE CRITERIA

### 3.1 First-Principles: The Three-Lever Control Model
Every microorganism needs three things to grow: suitable temperature, suitable pH, and available water[span_272](start_span)[span_272](end_span). Food preservation technology is fundamentally the science of pushing one or more of these three levers outside the microbial comfort zone[span_273](start_span)[span_273](end_span). This module is organized around those three levers, in the exact order the syllabus tests them: Temperature → pH → Water Activity[span_274](start_span)[span_274](end_span).

**MICROBIAL GROWTH REQUIRES:**[span_275](start_span)[span_275](end_span)
| TEMPERATURE (°C) | pH (H+ conc.) | WATER ACTIVITY (aw) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

Push any lever outside tolerance → growth slows/stops[span_276](start_span)[span_276](end_span).

### 3.2 Cardinal Parameter 1: Temperature Domains
| Microbial Group | Growth Range | Optimal Growth Temperature |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Psychrotrophs**[span_277](start_span)[span_277](end_span) | Grow well at ≤7°C[span_278](start_span)[span_278](end_span) | 20–30°C[span_279](start_span)[span_279](end_span) |
| **Mesophiles**[span_280](start_span)[span_280](end_span) | Grow well between 20–45°C[span_281](start_span)[span_281](end_span) | 30–40°C[span_282](start_span)[span_282](end_span) |
| **Thermophiles**[span_283](start_span)[span_283](end_span) | Grow well at ≥45°C[span_284](start_span)[span_284](end_span) | 55–65°C[span_285](start_span)[span_285](end_span) |

**Key nuance:** Psychrotrophs' optimal growth is NOT at refrigeration temperature — it is at 20–30°C[span_286](start_span)[span_286](end_span). Their defining feature is that they can still grow, albeit slowly, at 7°C or below — this is precisely why refrigeration only extends shelf life (by increasing lag phase) rather than fully halting spoilage[span_287](start_span)[span_287](end_span). Reducing temperature increases microbial lag time, which is the biochemical basis for chilling as a preservation method[span_288](start_span)[span_288](end_span).

<Callout type="danger" title="🚨 HPSC EXAM TRAP">
**Absolute qualifier:** *"Refrigeration completely stops microbial growth."* — FALSE[span_289](start_span)[span_289](end_span). It only slows growth (extended lag phase) — psychrotrophs remain active at chill temperatures, hence refrigerated shelf life is finite, not infinite[span_290](start_span)[span_290](end_span).
</Callout>
